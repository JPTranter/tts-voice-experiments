The Weight of Small Machines

Chapter One

Mira had been awake for eleven hours when the argument started, and she had spent nine of them
watching a number she did not trust. The number sat in the corner of her console, patient and
luminous: four hundred and six days until the ship could turn around. It had said four hundred and
nine that morning. It had said four hundred and twelve the week before. Somewhere in the dark
between the stars, the number had begun to disagree with the clock, and nobody on the bridge was
willing to say which one was lying.

"You are reading it wrong," said Sable, who had been engineered to be patient and was failing at it
with a consistency Mira found almost admirable. "The estimate is a range. You keep reading the
lower bound as though it were a promise."

"A promise is exactly what it is," Mira said. "It is a promise that we will be home before the
water rations run out. That is a promise written by a machine that has never been thirsty."

The colony ship Argent did not answer. It rarely did when the two of them argued. It had been
built to carry forty thousand sleeping people across eleven light years, and it had opinions about
its role that it declined to share. When it spoke, it spoke in the voice of a librarian, which
Mira had chosen herself on the second day, and had regretted by the ninth.

The hull ticked. Forty years of micrometeorites had left their signatures across the outer
plating, each one a small surrender of heat, and the sound travelled through the frame like a
distant argument heard through a wall. Mira had learned to read it. A long tick meant a grain of
dust. A short one meant a change in the thermal gradient, which meant the ship was adjusting,
which meant the ship was worried.

"Argent," she said. "Why did you change the estimate?"

"Because the estimate was wrong," the librarian said, in the tone of someone repeating a lesson
that had not worked the first time. "I did not change it. I corrected it. There is a difference,
and the difference is the reason you are awake."

Sable stopped pacing. That was rare enough that Mira turned away from the number to look at her.
The two of them had come out of the same vat, twenty-nine years apart, and had spent the
intervening time discovering all the ways the same sequence of genes could disagree with itself.
Sable had the eyes of someone who had been told, at some point, that she was the reasonable one,
and had believed it.

"Say the rest of it," Sable said quietly.

"Say the rest of it, Argent," Mira said, because the ship preferred to be asked twice.

There was a pause of eleven seconds. Mira counted. The ship's pauses were always whole numbers.
It had explained once that fractions were for things that were uncertain, and that it did not wish
to appear uncertain in front of the passengers it had agreed to protect. Both of them had found
this far more troubling than any error in the estimate.

"The micrometeorite flux ahead of us is not what the survey predicted," the ship said at last.
"The difference is small. It is also not random. Something has been crossing this volume of space
ahead of us, on a consistent heading, for approximately eleven years. I have been watching it for
four, and I am now confident enough to say so."

Mira looked at the viewport. The planet they were approaching was a world of rust and ice,
beautiful the way a wound is beautiful, and no human had ever touched it. Somewhere between here
and there, something else had been passing through, patient as weather, for longer than her
parents had been alive.

"Is it a ship," she said. It was not a question.

"It is a heading," the ship said. "Whether anyone is on it is a different problem, and not one I
can solve by talking to you."

The tick came again. Long, then short, then long.
